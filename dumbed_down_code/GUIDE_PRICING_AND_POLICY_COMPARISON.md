## 1. Setting Up the Empirical Counterfactual

In the empirical counterfactual, we model a firm that sells **Plain Yogurt** (the inside good $j$). The outside alternative $0$ represents **all other choices combined** (Berry yogurt, other yogurt, or no yogurt).

### Estimated Parameter Values (from Nielsen HomeScan/MarketScan)
From Table 2 of `mason_draft.pdf` (the no-habit 3-flavor demand estimation):
1. **Base Quality for Plain Yogurt**:
   $$\beta = \text{inside\_constant} + \beta_{\text{plain}} = 0.0162 - 1.3013 = -1.2851$$
2. **Mean Price Sensitivity**:
   $$E[\alpha] = -\exp\left(\mu_\alpha + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_\alpha^2\right) = -\exp\left(0.4834 + 0.5 \times 1.2474^2\right) = -3.5304$$
3. **Switching Distribution**:
   $$\gamma \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\gamma = -0.8382, \; \sigma_\gamma = 2.0992)$$
   - Notice that the mean $\mu_\gamma = -0.8382$ is negative! This indicates that on average, consumers exhibit **habit and brand loyalty** (switching incurs a cost).
   - However, the standard deviation $\sigma_\gamma = 2.0992$ is large, meaning there is wide heterogeneity: some consumers strongly dislike switching, while others strongly prefer variety.

## 2. Trimming and Quintile Discretization

Because a normal distribution has infinite tails, extreme outliers (e.g., $|\gamma| > 5$) would imply absurd willingness to pay or deep subsidies. 

To create realistic pricing policies:
1. **Trimming**: We trim the distribution at the **5th percentile** and **95th percentile**, retaining the central $90\%$ of the population.
2. **Renormalizing**: The retained $90\%$ probability mass is renormalized to $1.0$.
3. **Quintiles ($Q_1$ to $Q_5$)**: The retained distribution is partitioned into 5 equal-mass bins, each containing $20\%$ of the retained mass ($18\%$ of the original population):
   - **$Q_1$ (Strong Habit / Switching Cost)**: Mean $\gamma \approx -3.18$
   - **$Q_2$ (Moderate Habit)**: Mean $\gamma \approx -1.83$
   - **$Q_3$ (Mild Habit)**: Mean $\gamma \approx -0.84$
   - **$Q_4$ (Mild Variety-Seeking)**: Mean $\gamma \approx +0.15$
   - **$Q_5$ (Strong Variety-Seeking)**: Mean $\gamma \approx +1.50$

## 3. The Counterfactual Policies

We evaluate two distinct information structures:

### Policy A: Uniform Pricing (Uninformed Baseline)
- **Information**: The firm observes whether the consumer bought plain yogurt last week ($h_0 \in \{j, 0\}$ and $h_1 \in \{j, 0\}$).
- **Uncertainty**: The firm knows the population distribution of $\gamma$, but does **not** know the consumer's quintile.
- **Action**: All consumers with the same history receive the exact same price offer ($p_1(h_0)$, $p_2(j)$, $p_2(0)$).
- **Beliefs**: Fixed across periods; no Bayesian learning.

### Policy B: Price Discrimination (Quintile-Informed Counterfactual)
- **Information**: The firm observes history AND the consumer's switching quintile ($k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$).
- **Action**: The firm customizes the price path $(p_1^k, p_2^k(j), p_2^k(0))$ specifically for quintile $k$.
- **Beliefs**: Fixed within each quintile's conditional distribution.

## 4. Consumer Surplus Accounting: Why Zero-Outside ($CS^Z$)?

In your model, deterministic utility difference is:
$$v = \beta + \alpha p + q(h)\gamma, \quad q(0) = +1, \; q(j) = -1$$

When computing Consumer Surplus across periods, there are two possible definitions:

### 1. Original Utility Levels ($CS^O$)
Keeps $V_0 = \gamma$ after history $j$.
When $\gamma < 0$, switching from plain yogurt to outside imposes a penalty $\gamma < 0$.
For Quintile 1 ($\gamma \approx -3.18$), this negative penalty makes total consumer surplus near zero ($-0.000054$). When dividing by this tiny baseline, the percentage change explodes to **$-5172\%$**!

### 2. Zero-Outside Relative Surplus ($CS^Z$) — Used in `mason_draft.pdf`
Defines consumer surplus as the value of having Plain Yogurt available **relative to having only the outside option** at that history:
$$C(p, h; \gamma) = \frac{\log(1 + \exp(\beta + \alpha p + q(h)\gamma))}{-\alpha} \ge 0$$
This measure:
- Is strictly non-negative ($CS^Z \ge 0$ everywhere).
- Focuses directly on the surplus generated by the firm's product.
- Eliminates the $-5172\%$ artifact and produces the well-behaved, intuitive numbers reported in Table 3 of your draft.

## 6. Economic Interpretation of the Findings

### Why Does Price Discrimination Help Outside Consumers ($+6.74\%$ CS)?
Consumers who start outside and have strong habits ($Q_1$) are very reluctant to try plain yogurt. Under Uniform Pricing, the firm charges a single compromised price ($p_1 = \$0.41$).
Under Price Discrimination, the firm offers $Q_1$ an **introductory discount down to $\$0.055$**! This massive price drop expands consumption, increasing consumer surplus for $Q_1$ by $+142\%$, pulling up the entire outside group.

### Why Does Price Discrimination Hurt Inside Consumers ($-2.85\%$ CS)?
Consumers who start inside and have strong habits ($Q_1$) are reluctant to leave. Under Price Discrimination, the firm identifies their loyalty and **raises both period-1 and period-2 prices** to extract their surplus. Consumer surplus for inside $Q_1$ drops by $-16.56\%$.

### The Winner-and-Loser Pattern:
- **Price Discrimination does not unilaterally hurt consumers**: Outside consumers gain, while inside repeaters pay more.
- Total welfare increases for outside consumers ($+4.66\%$) and remains almost neutral for inside consumers ($-0.39\%$).
- The firm gains in both states ($+2.95\%$ and $+2.01\%$), confirming that information about switching types is strictly profitable for the firm.
